With the Spring/Summer 2027 shows complete, the industry's read on the season has settled on one word, maximalism, with Roberto Cavalli's Milan show as its loudest example and coverage of Paris describing a turn away from minimalism towards prints, charms, embellishment and colour. The runway data tells a more precise story. Based on The Data Fashion Brief's analysis of Paris Fashion Week SS27, 56 percent of the season's colour palette was still neutral, and the two aesthetics did not replace each other so much as stack: a quiet, nineties-leaning wardrobe underneath, with decoration, colour accents and oversized accessories doing the shouting on top. Maximalism is the one that got bigger and louder, but it did so on a base that quiet luxury built.

## The base stayed neutral, and most houses dressed it up

Black accounted for 21 percent of the Paris palette, white and cream for 17 percent, brown and beige for 11 percent and grey for 7 percent, which puts 56 percent of the season's colour weight in neutrals. Lanvin was 89 percent neutral, Celine 81 percent and Balenciaga 74 percent, and those three plus Tom Ford hold the season's black number up.

What sits on top of that base is where the volume lives. Across the 997 looks analysed, 31 percent carried ruffles, fringe, sequins, beading, embroidery, three-dimensional flowers, feathers or appliqué, and that reached every one of the 16 houses, with Dior at 70 percent of its looks, Chloé at 69 percent and Isabel Marant at 60 percent. Textured fabrics appeared in 18 percent of looks and metallic finishes reached 14 houses, while print worn on print stayed a house story, in 2 percent of looks and led by Dries Van Noten and Miu Miu. Fashionista's Paris roundup picked out the same surface, texture play through three-dimensional embellishments and florals pushed forward as dimensional appliqués, and Chloé shows the formula in one collection: 72 percent neutral by palette, 69 percent embellished by look.

## Not every show was Cavalli

The clearest statement of maximalism came from Milan, where Fausto Puglisi's Roberto Cavalli collection was described by WWD as a burst of fireworks on the runway. The Data Fashion Brief's separate decode of that show puts numbers on it: animal print made up 28 percent of the Cavalli palette, pink 16 percent, red 14 percent and gold 12 percent, and black fell to 6 percent. Set against Paris, where black is 21 percent of the palette and animal print 2 percent, that is a different season. The Paris houses closest to that register are the ones that opted out of the neutral base, Dries Van Noten at 22 percent neutral with graphic and floral print as its two biggest slices, Balmain at 34 percent with animal print as its largest slice, and Miu Miu at 35 percent. The season had a Cavalli end and a Lanvin end, and most houses sat in between.

## Colour came in quietly, and pink led it

Where colour entered the Paris palette it came as an accent, and pink is the clearest case. By palette weight it is only 5 percent of the season, but it appears in 8 percent of all looks and in 13 of the 16 houses. Isabel Marant gave pink 16 percent of its palette, more than three times the season average, in a pale, faded register that matches WWD's description of the collection as faded pastels, while Valentino's 13 percent was fuchsia and cerise. The shade that connects the most houses is ballet pink, in 17 looks across six collections, led by Stella McCartney and Chloé. Green was the biggest single colour at 8 percent but concentrated at Lacoste and Loewe, and the colours the press expected most were the ones Paris used least, red at 2 percent of the palette and purple at 1 percent.

## The quiet side has a nineties shape

The neutral base is not shapeless. The column was the silhouette of 35 percent of all Paris looks and reached every house, the pencil skirt was the only piece shown at all 16 houses, in 15 percent of looks, wool suiting was the season's biggest material, in a quarter of looks, and the slip dress reached 13 houses. That is the vocabulary of nineties minimalism, and the press named it as such, from W Magazine framing Louis Vuitton's Paris show as a new kind of minimalism to WWD describing Calvin Klein's New York collection as built on the brand's nineties archetypes, the pencil skirt, slip dressing, the trouser and tailoring. The season's drama came from surface and styling rather than shape, since draped silhouettes were 6 percent of looks and full ball gown volume only 1 percent.

## Accessories carry the volume, starting with sunglasses

Accessories are where the loud layer is most visible, and where coverage and buyers agree most. WWD's survey of buyers after the spring shows named belts the key statement piece of the season, big at Celine and triple at Gucci, second only to chunky jewellery and maxi sunglasses, which it described as enduring, and one buyer singled out Celine's round metal sunglasses. Show by show, Celine reworked its oversized oval sunglasses as rimless versions and sent out highlighter-yellow and bug-eyed styles with enormous belts, Miu Miu showed bug-eye frames crossed with a cat-eye shape in a parade of colours next to oversized bowling bags, Balmain put sunglasses on many of its models, and The Zoe Report's Paris roundup listed charm-adorned footwear and vivid floral accessories among the season's defining trends.

One caveat belongs here. The Data Fashion Brief's tagging covers the garments in each look, not eyewear, so the sunglasses finding rests on the buyers' survey and the show reviews rather than on a runway count, a gap worth stating rather than filling with an estimate.

## What this means for brands and retailers

The season does not support the headline that maximalism has killed quiet luxury, and it does not support the opposite either. In Paris the base of most collections is still neutral, straight and tailored, which is the wardrobe the quiet luxury customer was buying all along, and what changed is the decoration, colour and accessory volume layered on top of it. For brands, that makes the two aesthetics one assortment rather than two customers: the neutral column or wool pencil skirt is the entry point, and the newness sits in what is sold with it, the embellished piece, the pink accent, the oversized frame. For retailers it argues for buying the quiet base in depth and the loud layer in breadth, since the loud layer is where houses differ most.

Search data points the same way for now. According to The Data Fashion Brief's analysis of Google Trends, worldwide interest in "quiet luxury" reached its high for the year in May and had fallen to less than a third of that level by August, a label losing its grip without the wardrobe behind it going anywhere. The real test comes when the Spring/Summer 2027 deliveries reach stores next year. One season of runway data is a signal, not a verdict, but the signal is consistent: the base is quiet, the top is loud, and the top is where the newness is.
