# FashionUnited: maximalism and the quiet base (draft 1, text only)

   Standfirst: The Data Fashion Brief's analysis of Paris Fashion Week finds maximalism bigger and louder than a season ago, and quiet luxury still dressing the base of most collections.

---

With the Spring/Summer 2027 shows complete, the industry's read on the season has settled on a single word, maximalism, with Roberto Cavalli's Milan show as its loudest example and press coverage of Paris describing a turning point away from minimalism towards prints, charms, embellishment and colour. The runway data tells a more precise story. Based on The Data Fashion Brief's analysis of Paris Fashion Week SS27, 56 percent of the season's colour palette was still neutral, and the two aesthetics did not replace each other so much as stack: a quiet, nineties-leaning wardrobe underneath, with decoration, colour accents and oversized accessories doing the shouting on top. Maximalism is the one that got bigger and louder this season, but it did so on a base that quiet luxury built.

## The base stayed neutral, and most houses dressed it up

Black alone accounted for 21 percent of the Paris palette, white and cream for 17 percent, brown and beige for 11 percent and grey for 7 percent, which together put 56 percent of the season's colour weight in neutrals. That base is not evenly spread. Lanvin was 89 percent neutral, with half of its entire palette in black, Celine was 81 percent and Balenciaga 74 percent, and those three plus Tom Ford are the houses holding the season's black number up, each with black at a third or more of its palette.

What sits on top of that base is where the season's volume lives. Across the 997 looks analysed, 31 percent carried at least one of ruffles, fringe, sequins, beading, embroidery, three-dimensional flowers, feathers or appliqué, and that reached every one of the 16 houses. Dior put embellishment on 70 percent of its looks, Chloé on 69 percent and Isabel Marant on 60 percent. Textured fabrics appeared in 18 percent of all looks, metallic finishes reached 14 of the 16 houses, and the most extreme version, print worn on print, stayed a house story rather than a season one, in only 2 percent of looks and led by Dries Van Noten and Miu Miu.

The press read the same split. Fashionista's Paris roundup picked out texture play through three-dimensional embellishments, netting and crinkled fabrics, with florals pushed forward as dimensional appliqués, and Who What Wear reported that Chanel, Loewe and Dries Van Noten leaned into unexpected embellishments, colour combinations and playful textures. Chloé is the house where the readings diverge most clearly: Whitewall described three-dimensional embroidered flowers and broderie anglaise, while Forbes read the same show as restrained colour and refined construction rather than excessive embellishment. The data says both are right, since Chloé is 72 percent neutral by palette and 69 percent embellished by look, which is the season's formula in a single collection.

## Not every show was Cavalli

The clearest statement of maximalism this season came from Milan, where Fausto Puglisi's Roberto Cavalli collection, titled House of Prints, was described by WWD as a burst of fireworks on the runway and by other reviewers as deliberately maximalist, with leopard, zebra and tiger mixed rather than used one at a time. The Data Fashion Brief's separate decode of that show puts numbers on it: animal print alone made up 28 percent of the Cavalli palette, pink took 16 percent, red 14 percent and gold 12 percent, and black fell to 6 percent. Printed silk chiffon and the long slip or column gown each appeared in 28 percent of its looks.

Set against the Paris average, that is a different season. Black is 21 percent of the Paris palette and 6 percent of Cavalli's, and animal print is 2 percent of the Paris palette against 28 percent at Cavalli. The Paris houses that come closest to that register are the ones that opted out of the neutral base altogether: Dries Van Noten, only 22 percent neutral and the most colourful house in Paris, with graphic and floral print as its two biggest slices, Balmain at 34 percent neutral with animal print as its single largest slice at 24 percent, and Miu Miu at 35 percent. At the other end, Lanvin, Celine and Balenciaga ran collections that were three quarters neutral or more. The season had a Cavalli end and a Lanvin end, and most houses sat in between, with a neutral base and decoration on it.

## Colour came in quietly, and pink led it

Where colour did enter the Paris palette, it did so as an accent rather than a statement, and pink is the clearest case. By palette weight pink is only 5 percent of the season, but it appears in 8 percent of all looks and in 13 of the 16 houses, which means a lot of outfits carried a little pink without being built around it. Isabel Marant gave pink 16 percent of its palette, more than three times the season average, and Valentino 13 percent, and they are not the same pink: Isabel Marant's is pale and faded, which matches WWD's description of the collection as faded pastels inspired by the hazy light of Los Angeles and southern Europe, while Valentino's is fuchsia and cerise. The shade that connects the most houses is ballet pink, in 17 looks across six collections, led by Stella McCartney and Chloé, with Dior, Balenciaga and Valentino behind them. Forbes picked out the same soft register, a dusty pink alongside mint and biscuit at Stella McCartney.

Green and olive was the season's biggest single colour at 8 percent, but it was concentrated at Lacoste, 24 percent of its palette, and Loewe, 20 percent, and the colours the press expected most were the ones Paris used least: red fell to 2 percent of the palette after New York's red season, and purple, which coverage of New York called the colour of the season, reached just 1 percent of the Paris palette at five houses.

## The quiet side has a nineties shape

The neutral base is not shapeless. The column was the overall silhouette of 35 percent of all Paris looks and reached every house, with Chanel and Isabel Marant both at 60 percent of their collections, and the two biggest pieces of the season were the pencil skirt, in 15 percent of looks and the only piece shown at all 16 houses, and the straight trouser at 13 percent. Wool suiting was the season's biggest material, in a quarter of all looks across 14 houses. The slip dress reached 13 houses, and lingerie-style tops, bandeaus, corsets, bodysuits and camisoles taken together, appeared in 12 percent of all looks across 14 houses, led by Stella McCartney and Valentino at around 30 percent of their collections each.

That is the vocabulary of nineties minimalism, the slip, the column, the pencil skirt and the suit, and the press named it as such. WWD's review of Lemaire's spring collection described oh-so-nineties slipdresses, W Magazine framed Louis Vuitton's Paris show as a new kind of minimalism, and in New York, WWD described Calvin Klein's collection as built on the brand's nineties archetypes, the pencil skirt, slip dressing, the trouser, tailoring and the trenchcoat. The maximalism of the season sits on top of that silhouette rather than replacing it: the drama in Paris came from fabric, surface and styling, since draped shapes were 6 percent of looks, fit-and-flare and the cinched waist around 3 percent each and full ball gown volume only 1 percent.

## Accessories carry the volume, starting with sunglasses

If the base is quiet and the surface is loud, accessories are where the loud part is most visible, and that is where the season's coverage and the buyers agree most. WWD's survey of buyers after the spring shows named belts as the key statement piece of the season, big at Celine and triple at Gucci, second only to chunky jewellery and maxi sunglasses, which it described as enduring, and one buyer singled out Celine's round metal sunglasses as a must-have. Bloomingdale's called embellishment, particularly beading and wood, a major trend across bags, shoes and jewellery, and Nordstrom listed maximalist jewellery among its top trends.

The eyewear reads the same way show by show. At Celine, Michael Rider reworked the house's oversized oval sunglasses as rimless versions and sent out highlighter-yellow and bug-eyed styles alongside enormous belts with equally enormous buckles, and reviewers described the accessories as there to throw a polished outfit slightly off balance rather than to complete it. Miu Miu showed bug-eye frames crossed with a cat-eye shape, in a parade of colours, next to oversized leather bowling bags and snakeskin-effect clutches. Balmain put sunglasses on many of its models, and The Zoe Report's Paris roundup, which carried embellished accessories in its headline, listed charm-adorned footwear and vivid floral accessories among the season's defining trends, with embellished sandals and crystal platforms at Dior, Dries Van Noten and Loewe, and stilettos with brooches and gold chains at Saint Laurent, where big earrings were part of the formula too.

One caveat belongs here. The Data Fashion Brief's tagging covers the garments in each look, not eyewear, so the sunglasses finding rests on the buyers' survey and the show reviews rather than on a runway count. The one accessory the Paris tagging does record, a visible necklace, appeared in 8 percent of looks, which is consistent with a season where the jewellery was noticed for its size rather than its frequency. That is a gap worth stating plainly rather than filling with an estimate.

## What this means for brands and retailers

The season does not support the headline that maximalism has killed quiet luxury, and it does not support the opposite either. In Paris, the base of most collections is still neutral, straight and tailored, and that is the part of the wardrobe the quiet luxury customer was buying all along. What changed is the amount of decoration, colour and accessory volume layered on top of it, and that is where the growth in maximalism actually sits: embellishment on a third of all looks at every house, pink in 13 of 16 collections, and eyewear, belts and jewellery scaled up across the shows that buyers singled out.

For brands, the implication is that the two aesthetics are one assortment rather than two customers. A neutral column or a wool pencil skirt is the entry point, and the margin and the newness sit in what is sold with it, the embellished piece, the pink accent, the oversized frame. For retailers, it argues for buying the quiet base in depth and the loud layer in breadth, since the loud layer is where houses differ most, from Cavalli's animal print to Isabel Marant's pale pink to Celine's hardware.

Search data points the same way for now. According to The Data Fashion Brief's analysis of Google Trends, worldwide interest in "quiet luxury" reached its high for the year in May and had fallen to less than a third of that level by August, which fits a term losing its grip as a label without the wardrobe behind it going anywhere. The next test is the Pre-Fall and Resort 2027 collections, and then the moment the Spring/Summer 2027 deliveries reach stores next year, which will show whether shoppers buy the decorated layer at the rate the runway showed it. One season of runway data is a signal, not a verdict, but the signal is consistent: the base is quiet, the top is loud, and the top is where the money is moving.
