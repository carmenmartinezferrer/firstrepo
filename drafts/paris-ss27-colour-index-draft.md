# Stop Calling Everything a Trend: Paris Colour Edition

## Paris dressed in black and cream, and let a handful of houses do all the colour

I promised you a proper colour post in the Paris trend report, so here it is! And honestly, Paris was a funny one for colour, because if you only look at the season as a whole it reads as the most sensible palette imaginable, black, cream, a bit of camel, and then you open up the individual houses and half of them are doing something completely different. So this piece is about that gap, what the season looks like from far away, and where the colour actually lives once you zoom in, house by house.

## Methodology, the short version

Every look from 16 Paris shows was split across its main colours (up to three per look) by how much of the outfit each one covers, so a black jacket over a red skirt counts as half black and half red, and every palette below adds up to 100%. All the shades of one colour sit in one slice (every pink is "pink"), and different colours are never merged, so red is never quietly counted as orange. The full detail lives at the bottom.

## The season palette

[CHART: season donut, donut-season.png]

Black leads at 21%, white and cream are right behind it at 17%, and brown and beige take another 11%, which with grey at 7% means more than half of Paris (56%) sits in neutrals. That's not a shock for Paris, I know, but it's worth saying out loud because it sets the bar for everything else: anything above about 5% of the palette is a real colour story this season, not a rounding error.

After the neutrals, green and olive is the biggest actual colour at 8%, then pink, blue and yellow at 5% each, navy at 4%, and the prints (floral, graphic and animal) adding up to around 9% between them. Red, which New York was so obsessed with, is down at 2%.

### The neutral houses, and the ones that ignored the memo

Some houses took "neutral" very literally. Lanvin is 89% neutral (half of its whole palette is black, and another third is white and cream), Celine is 81% and Balenciaga 74%, and those three plus Tom Ford are the houses holding the season's black number up, each with black at a third or more of their palette, against 21% for the season.

On the other end, Dries Van Noten is only 22% neutral, the most colourful house in Paris by a distance, and most of that colour isn't even a single colour, it's print, graphic print (17%) and floral print (15%) are its two biggest slices. Balmain (34% neutral, and its single biggest slice is animal print at 24%, more than its black!) and Miu Miu (35%) are the other two houses that basically opted out of the neutral base.

## House by house

[CHART: house donut grid, rows of 4, grid-donuts-all.png (or grid-donuts-1-of-2.png and grid-donuts-2-of-2.png as two images)]

[CHART: swatch grid, top five colours per house, grid-swatches.png]

I'm not going to walk you through all 16 (you have the donuts for that, and they're honestly more fun to look at than to read about), but a few of them tell a really clear story on their own:

- **Lacoste** is the house that wrote its colour story into the invitation. The show was called "Dress Code: No White Required", built around [the green in the house's identity, the red clay of the court and flashes of electric yellow](https://schonmagazine.com/lacoste-ss27-no-white-required/), and the data matches that almost word for word: green is its biggest slice at 24% (mostly khaki and bottle green), then orange and rust at 12% (terracotta and clay, literally), then yellow at 11%, which is mostly chartreuse. The one funny bit is that white and cream is still 20% of its palette, so white was very much not required, but it came anyway.
- **Miu Miu** went navy and brown, navy is its biggest slice at 21% (five times the season average) and brown and beige is right behind at 20%, mostly chocolate, brown check and chestnut leather. And it barely used black at all, 6%, which for a Paris show is quite a statement.
- **Chanel, Maison Margiela and Dior** are the grey houses, at 15%, 16% and 13% of their palettes against 7% for the season. At Chanel the grey is mostly checks and charcoal (very tweed), at Dior it's flannel grey.
- **Chloé and Isabel Marant** are the cream houses, 41% and 35% white and cream, and both pair it with soft pink rather than with black.

## The colour deep dive

### Pink is the one I'd actually watch

[CHART: pink by house vs season average, bar chart, 06-pink-by-house.png from the trend report set]

Pink is only 5% of the season palette, but it reached 11 of the 16 houses' palettes, and when you count every look with any pink in it at all, it's in 8% of all looks across 13 houses. So it's less of a statement colour and more of a little bit of pink everywhere, which honestly is how most of us actually wear it.

Isabel Marant (16% of its palette, more than three times the season average) and Valentino (13%) are where it's concentrated, and here's the part I find really interesting, they're not the same pink at all. Isabel Marant's pink is mostly pale pink, very soft and faded, while Valentino's is fuchsia and cerise, properly loud. The shade that connects the most houses is ballet pink, in 17 looks across six of them, led by Stella McCartney and Chloé, with Dior, Balenciaga and Valentino in there too. So if you're asking me which pink, it's ballet pink for the season and fuchsia if you want to dress like Valentino.

> **In The Press**
> [WWD's review of Isabel Marant](https://wwd.com/runway/spring-2027/paris/isabel-marant/review/) describes the collection as faded pastels inspired by the hazy light of Los Angeles and southern Europe in summer, with butterfly belt buckles in a soft shell pink, which is exactly the pale, washed-out pink the data picks out at that house rather than a bright one.

### Green, but two very different greens

Green and olive is the season's biggest real colour at 8%, and it's in 15 of the 16 palettes, but the story is really two houses, Lacoste at 24% and Loewe at 20%, with Balmain (14%) and Tom Ford (12%) behind them. And just like pink, the two leaders aren't wearing the same thing. Lacoste's green is dark, khaki and bottle green, while Loewe's is light and fresh, jade and pistachio mint, and Tom Ford's is a loden olive. So "green is in" is true, but which green very much depends on whose collection you're looking at.

### Navy and grey, the quiet ones

Navy only reached 9 of the palettes and sits at 4% for the season, but Miu Miu built 21% of its palette around it and Tom Ford 11%, which makes navy one of the most concentrated colours in Paris. Grey works the same way, 7% for the season but double that at Chanel, Margiela and Dior.

> **In The Press**
> Here's where the data and the coverage only half agree. The [Miu Miu reviews](https://theimpression.com/miu-miu-spring-2027-fashion-show-review/) I found list navy, shades of rose, orange, red, light green, yellow and purple as the palette, and Miuccia Prada said she wanted the colours to feel elegant rather than like sportswear colours. Navy matches perfectly, but brown, which is 20% of Miu Miu's palette in the data, barely gets a mention. My guess is that brown reads as a neutral to most reviewers, so it doesn't register as a "colour", but in this data it's the second biggest slice of the whole collection.

### Yellow, and where the butter went

Yellow and gold is 5% of the season, led by Loewe (15%, canary and lemon sorbet) and Lacoste (11%, chartreuse). But I have to flag something here, because I know a lot of you are still on the butter yellow thing (me too). Butter shows up in 18 looks in Paris, most of them at Balenciaga, but the data files it under white and cream, not yellow, so it's hidden inside that 17% rather than in the yellow slice. Which tells you something in itself, I think, butter has gone so pale this season that it's basically being read as a cream.

### And red?

Red is 2% of the season, the smallest real colour in Paris after purple and burgundy. Chanel (mostly scarlet) and Lanvin (lacquer red) give it 7% each, and the most common red shade across the whole week is lipstick red, with Tom Ford wearing the most of it. After New York's red moment, Paris basically didn't follow, and I think that's worth saying rather than pretending red is everywhere.

## The Takeaway

What Paris SS27 actually looked like in colour:

- A neutral season overall, 56% black, white and cream, grey and brown, with Lanvin, Celine, Balenciaga and Tom Ford doing most of the black.
- Pink is the most widespread colour beyond the neutrals, in a little bit of almost everything, with ballet pink as the shade that crosses houses, and Isabel Marant (soft) and Valentino (loud) as the two houses really leaning in.
- Green is the biggest single colour, but it's two different greens, dark at Lacoste and fresh at Loewe.
- Navy and grey are concentrated colours, navy at Miu Miu and Tom Ford, grey at Chanel, Margiela and Dior.
- Butter yellow is still around, it's just gone pale enough to be filed under cream, and red stayed in New York.

So, the ballet pink or the bottle green? Tell me which one you'd actually wear, because I already know my answer and it's going to cost me money.

I hope you enjoyed it, and let me know in the comments anything you would like to know, doubts, if there's anything else you would like to know! I read you.

## Full methodology

Sixteen houses from Paris Fashion Week Spring Summer 2027, 997 looks, every show complete: Balenciaga, Balmain, Celine, Chanel, Chloé, Dior, Dries Van Noten, Isabel Marant, Lacoste, Lanvin, Loewe, Maison Margiela, Miu Miu, Stella McCartney, Tom Ford and Valentino. This is the same dataset as the Paris trend report.

Every look is split across its main colours, up to three, by how much of the outfit each covers, so palettes are shares of total colour across the looks and each one adds up to 100%. All shades of one colour family sit in one slice, and the slice colour on the donuts is a blend of those shades. Different colour families are never merged, and prints (floral, graphic, animal and other) are their own slices. Neutral means black, white and cream, grey, and brown and beige. A colour counts as over-indexing at a house when its share of that house's palette is at least one and a half times the season average.

The "8% of looks" figure for pink counts every look with any pink in it, which is a different question from pink's 5% share of the palette, and both are in the text on purpose.

Hex codes are read from runway images, some measured from pixels and most estimated by eye, so treat the exact hexes as approximate. The colour families themselves are reliable. Slices that round to 0% for a house are left off its donut.
