# Every Designer Is Suddenly Making the Same Asian-Inspired Jacket (And There Is a Very 2026 Reason Why)

*Mandarin collars, frog closures and embroidered satin were all over the SS27 runways, so I looked at where the look comes from, why it is back and whether it will last.*

There is one kind of jacket I keep seeing, and once you notice it you cannot stop. The pieces I see most are Conner Ives's satin coats, embroidered with cranes, wisteria and blossom and trimmed in fur, in ivory, gold and beige.

Then the SS27 runways did their own version. Ralph Lauren showed an ivory jacquard cutaway with a mandarin collar, Christian Cowan cropped it in off-white with a black high collar and cord frog closures, The Garment made a soft off-white version with the same closures, and Elie Saab did it in olive with floral embroidery. It is not only China either, because Alberta Ferretti's version comes with kimono-style sleeves, so the reference is East Asian dress more broadly.

FashionUnited's edit of the trend shows it at eleven houses, seven with a mandarin collar and four as jackets, and eight of the eleven put it in white, off-white, ivory, champagne or pale yellow in place of the expected reds and golds.

## WHAT THE SEARCHES SAY

The year starts with Lunar New Year. Searches for "qipao" and "opera jacket" both hit their highest point of the year in the week of 15 February 2026, which is the usual seasonal moment for Chinese dress.

What is not usual is what happened next, which is Chinamaxxing. That is the TikTok name for adopting Chinese habits as a lifestyle, with people filming themselves wearing house slippers indoors, swapping iced drinks for warm water with goji berries and trying Tai Chi, usually with the catchphrase "You've met me at a very Chinese time in my life". As a search it did not exist until early February, and it went from zero to its peak in two weeks, by 22 February, before losing two thirds of that the week after.

Then the jacket took over. Searches for "mandarin jacket" peaked in the week of 12 April, seven weeks after the meme, and stayed close to that level from late March to mid May, 159% above where they sat last year. "Opera jacket" had a second bump in May too, and "qipao" stayed high right through August, so people kept the clothes and dropped the joke.

[CHART: 01-search-jump.png, % rise in Google searches at this year's peak, vs last year]

Since then the meme has lost 85% of its searches and the jacket 68%, although jacket searches did pick up again in August and September, right on the shows.

[CHART: 02-search-since-peak.png, % fall in Google searches since this year's peak]

Today jacket searches sit 16% below last year. What is rising is the designer end, because searches for Conner Ives jumped in the week of the New York show in September, hit their highest point of the year in late October, and are now 158% above last year.

[CHART: 03-search-now.png, % change in Google searches, this year vs last year]

## THE HISTORY

The mandarin collar jacket descends from the magua, a riding jacket worn by Manchu horsemen that became official dress under the Qing dynasty. The modern version is called a tangzhuang, and the "Tang" has nothing to do with the Tang dynasty, it is simply the word that came to mean "Chinese" abroad.

Western fashion keeps coming back to it. Yves Saint Laurent built his 1977 "Les Chinoises" collection around it without ever having visited Asia, Tom Ford paid tribute to that collection in his last show for the house in 2004, and in 2015 the Met's "China: Through the Looking Glass" drew 815,992 visitors, a record for the Costume Institute at the time. Each of those was the "Oriental" version, a Western fantasy of Asia that was rich and theatrical and closer to a costume than to clothes.

## WHY NOW

The first reason is that this time it starts in Asia. Young Chinese consumers have been wearing "new Chinese style" for a few years, mixing knot buttons and traditional cuts into everyday outfits, and China Daily counted over 2.6 million posts about it on Xiaohongshu by early 2024. Adidas showed a Tang-style track jacket at Shanghai Fashion Week in October 2025, and it travelled west from there.

The second is the mood in the West. Time read Chinamaxxing as a growing fascination with China as its soft power rises, although other writers think it says more about young Westerners being tired of their own politics, and I suspect it is a bit of both.

The third is maximalism, and this is the one I think explains why it is trending this much. The version people are actually wearing is not the plain mandarin jacket, it is the Asian-inspired piece with everything else piled on top: satin and jacquard, heavy embroidery, fur collars, fur or feather cuffs. That is exactly the dressed-up, more-is-more mood I have been writing about [NEEDS DATA: link to your maximalism piece], where Lectra's tracking of European brands had sequins up 40% and fringe up 34% on last summer and the analysts read it as a demand for escapism. Traditional Chinese dress is wonderfully theatrical to begin with (think of the opera costumes, the embroidery, the birds and the flowers), so it gives maximalism a ready-made shape. This part is my own reading and my own Pinterest board, not a data point, but once you see the fur and the jacquard together you cannot unsee it.

[IMAGE: 07-asianmaxxing-moodboard.png, eight looks from my Pinterest board "Asianmaxxing aesthetic", Asian-inspired jackets and coats in satin, jacquard and velvet with fur trim and embroidery]

The fourth is nostalgia, which is also my own reading. After seasons of quiet luxury people want clothes with character and a past, and a shape fashion has loved since 1977 gives them exactly that. Conner Ives sits at the maximalist end of this, all embroidery and fur, while the runways took the same references and made them quiet.

[IMAGE: 06-traditional-chinese-dress-met.png, six Chinese theatrical jackets and women's robes from the 18th to early 20th century, The Met, public domain]

## WHAT THIS MEANS FOR YOU

**If you run a brand or design for one**, make it quiet and wearable like the runways did, in pale neutrals with one soft accent, and credit it properly (the jacket is a tangzhuang and the closures are pankou), because the brands being criticised are the ones selling it with no mention of where it comes from.

**If you are a buyer**, the simple satin version had its peak in spring. I would buy narrow on that and put the money into the next step, which is the tailored jacket and the longer embroidered coat.

**If you are a customer**, you do not need a new one! This shape has been made for decades, so a vintage one is the same trend, and if you bought yours in spring, the designers are only now catching up with you.

So is this one more Western fantasy of Asia, or the first time the trend is actually being led from there? I think it is the second, which is why I expect it to last longer than the meme did.
