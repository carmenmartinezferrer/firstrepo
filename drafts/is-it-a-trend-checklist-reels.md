# Reels: "Is It a Trend, or Just One House Having a Moment?"

**What the article argues.** This is about my process, not about any one trend: before I call something a trend and not a moment I run it through a short list of checks. The Reels condense the article's six questions into the four things I do first (count two numbers, put it on the same scale as its rival, wait for the spike to pass, find an independent source), with the other two folded in (the average hiding a few loud houses sits inside "two numbers", and "if the data can't say what it is, it stays unverified" becomes the rule at the end). The examples (tweed, red, slip dress against midi skirt, skinny jean, embellishment) are there only as proof that each step catches something.

Three Reels with different angles and a different order of peaks: the four things in one Reel, then the first two in depth (the same-scale step as a Master Reel, the two-numbers step as a Comparison Reveal). All three close by sending people to the article with the keyword CHECKLIST, the same mechanism as your PARIS Reels.

**Full script (read-through):**
Every season someone sends me a list of trends, and before I believe it I do four things. That's how I tell a trend from a moment, and each one has caught a fake. First, I count two numbers, how many houses wear it and how much of each collection it is, because tweed was 5% of Paris looks but 42% of Chanel's, so it's a signature and not a trend. Second, I put it next to its rival on the same scale, because the slip dress beat the midi skirt in search in 50 of the last 53 weeks, even though the midi was at every single house. Third, I wait for the spike to pass, because skinny jean searches jumped in the spring and were back at last year's level by October. And fourth, I look for a source that doesn't depend on the first one, because embellishment was in 31% of Paris looks, retail tracking had sequins up 40%, and a forecast had fringe skirts up 71% in searches, three methods pointing one way. Pass most of them and it's a trend, pass one and it's a moment, and if the data can't say what the thing is, it stays unverified. Comment CHECKLIST for the full list.

## Reel 2: Master DFB Formula, "The step that finds the story" (about 45s)

**Angle:** the single step in my process that does the most work is putting everything on one scale, because something can look strong alone and barely register next to something else.

**Full script (read-through):**
The one step that finds the story in my data is putting everything on the same scale. Because a trend can look strong alone and still barely register next to another one. In New York the midi skirt was at every single house, so on the runway it looks like the winner. But on Google's own scale the slip dress ran higher in 50 of the last 53 weeks, and outside the show spike it averaged 25 against 9. I do the same to forecasts, where one report had 24 items and six different kinds of number, with a +21% sitting next to a +888% and nothing measured twice. Only when I group items by what they share can I see what's actually moving. So when someone shows you one big number, ask what it's sitting next to. Comment CHECKLIST for the other three things I check.

## Reel 3: Comparison Reveal, "Tweed or denim, which one is the real trend?" (about 45s)
