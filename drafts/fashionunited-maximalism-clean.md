# FashionUnited: maximalism (version 14, short version after the editor's comments of 30 October)

## Title

**Maximalism got bigger, and the data says it carries into Fall/Winter 2027**

Standfirst: Runway tagging across Spring/Summer 2027 finds embellishment on a third of all looks analysed, texture on nearly a fifth and bold colour accents in most collections, and the fabric fairs and forecasts for next season point the same way.

---

With the Spring/Summer 2027 shows complete and the Fall/Winter 2027 buying season about to open, the industry's read on the season has settled on one word, maximalism, with Roberto Cavalli's Milan show as its loudest example and coverage of Paris describing a turn towards prints, charms, embellishment and colour. Based on The Data Fashion Brief's analysis of Paris Fashion Week SS27, decoration appeared at all 16 houses analysed, colour arrived as an accent in most, and accessories carried the loudest part of the look. The question for buyers is not whether maximalism is here, but how big it got, which version of it is selling, and whether it holds into next season.

## Maximalism is bigger, and it is on the runway

Across the 997 Paris looks analysed from 16 shows, 31 percent carried ruffles, fringe, sequins, beading, embroidery, three-dimensional flowers, feathers or appliqué, and that reached every one of the 16 houses, with Dior, Chloé and Isabel Marant each showing it on more than 60 percent of their looks. Textured fabrics appeared in 18 percent of looks and metallic finishes reached 14 houses.

[Image: Dior, Chloé or Isabel Marant SS27, embellished looks]

The clearest statement came from Milan, where the Roberto Cavalli collection was reviewed as a burst of fireworks on the runway. The Data Fashion Brief's decode of that show puts numbers on it: animal print made up 28 percent of the Cavalli palette, pink 16 percent, red 14 percent and gold 12 percent, and black fell to 6 percent. In Paris, the houses closest to that register are the ones that opted out of a neutral base, Dries Van Noten at 22 percent neutral with graphic and floral print as its two biggest slices, and Balmain at 34 percent with animal print as its single largest slice.

[Image: Roberto Cavalli SS27, Dries Van Noten SS27, Balmain SS27]

*Methodology: The Data Fashion Brief built an AI-assisted tool that scans every look from the Paris Fashion Week SS27 collections and tags it by garment type, fit, fabric, finish, colour and detail. Each trend is measured two ways: how dominant it is within a single designer's own collection, and how present it is across the whole season. Colour shares split every look across up to three colours by how much of the outfit each one covers, so each palette adds up to 100 percent, and neutral means black, white and cream, grey, and brown and beige. The houses covered: Balenciaga, Balmain, Celine, Chanel, Chloé, Dior, Dries Van Noten, Isabel Marant, Lacoste, Lanvin, Loewe, Maison Margiela, Miu Miu, Stella McCartney, Tom Ford and Valentino, 997 looks in total. Search data comes from Google Trends, worldwide, 26 October 2025 to 25 October 2026, compared with the preceding year.*

## Why maximalism is working now

The runway is responding to a customer who has changed what luxury is for. The State of Fashion: Face to Face With Luxury Clients, a survey of more than 2,000 luxury clients in the United States and China published in June by The Business of Fashion and McKinsey, found the appeal of luxury shifting away from status and visibility towards identity, connection and self-expression, with status signalling and logo recognition in decline in the United States in particular. The same report series finds that 81 percent of luxury customers under 35 name design and creativity as their main reason to buy. A customer buying to express something rather than to signal wealth has a reason to want colour, print and decoration.

Retail and search data say the same thing. Lectra's assortment tracking across European brands for summer 2026 recorded sequins up 40 percent and fringe up 34 percent year on year, which its analysts read as a demand for escapism and individuality. According to The Data Fashion Brief's analysis of Google Trends, worldwide searches for "chunky belt" averaged almost double their level of a year earlier, up 93 percent, and "80s luxury", shorthand for bigger shoulders and chunkier gold jewellery, rose from almost nothing, up more than 400 percent on a small base. Both terms peaked in the spring and have settled back since, but both closed the year well above where they started.

Price has done some of the work too. Luxury prices rose 61 percent on average between 2019 and 2025, according to the same State of Fashion research, and Bain's outlook for 2026 describes a sector returning to growth of 3 to 5 percent while warning that those increases left aspirational shoppers feeling let down. A shopper who can no longer justify a plain cashmere piece at a luxury price has little reason to keep dressing in that uniform, and the houses growing with decoration, colour and visible craft are selling that shopper something that looks like what it costs.

The shift reaches beyond fashion. The interior designer Barbara Reimelt says she has tired of the matched set of curved sofa and curved chairs and wants pieces that almost do not go together, a sofa with corners next to a vintage or mid-century chair, because a piece that has had a life before brings a character new furniture rarely has, and The Data Fashion Brief's Google Trends data shows worldwide searches for "maximalist interior" running about half as high again as a year earlier on average. Mismatched rather than matched, lived-in rather than new, with one deep colour as the accent, is the same maximalism as texture and character that the fabric fairs are now showing for next season.

## Accessories carry the loudest part

Accessories are where the maximalist half of the season is most visible, and where coverage and buyers agree most. Buyers surveyed by WWD after the spring shows named belts the season's key statement piece, big at Celine and triple at Gucci, second only to chunky jewellery and maxi sunglasses. Celine reworked its oversized oval sunglasses as rimless versions and sent out highlighter yellow and bug-eyed styles with enormous belts, Miu Miu showed bug-eye frames crossed with a cat-eye shape in a parade of colours next to oversized bowling bags, and Balmain put sunglasses on many of its models.

[Image: Celine SS27 sunglasses and belt; Miu Miu SS27 bug-eye frames]

## What carries into Fall/Winter 2027

Decoration carries, colour does not. Première Vision Paris, the fabric fair for Autumn/Winter 2027/28 held in September, built the season on texture, irregularity and visible craft, with a micro-theme of metallic-thread tweeds and sequin-embroidered laces and a palette of deep purples, bordeaux and mineral neutrals in place of this summer's brights. The forecasters put numbers on the same direction. Heuritech reports fringe detailing up 17 percent year on year in July 2026 with a further rise forecast for spring 2027, and expects adoption of large sequins to grow 23 percent over the next two years. Trendalytics groups its Fall/Winter 2027 womenswear forecast under clashing prints, fringe and tactile textures, framed as a reaction against corporate minimalism and AI-generated imagery.

So the Spring/Summer 2027 version, pink, embellished sandals and bug-eye sunglasses, hands over to a heavier one, fringe, fur-effect volume, sequins on tweed and lace, and a darker palette, with accessories still doing the work.

## What this means for brands and retailers

For Spring/Summer 2027 deliveries, the data supports buying the decorated layer with confidence, embellishment at every house, pink as the accent colour with green and blue behind it, oversized belts, jewellery and eyewear, while keeping plain basics in depth, since the Paris houses themselves split into a bold camp and a basic camp and very few tried to do both at once. For Fall/Winter 2027, the signal is to shift the maximalist budget from bright colour to surface: fringe, sequins on tweed and lace, texture and visible craft, in purples, bordeaux and deep neutrals.

One season of runway data is a signal, but the signal is consistent across the runway, retail assortments, search and now the fabric fairs. Maximalism is bigger than it was, and its next season looks heavier rather than quieter. The first test comes at the January trade fairs, when buyers commit to it in cloth.
