---

With the Spring/Summer 2027 shows complete, the industry's read on the season has settled on one word: maximalism, with Roberto Cavalli's Milan show as its loudest example and Paris coverage describing a turn toward prints, charms, embellishment, and colour. The demand is real, and the runway reflects it. But the data does not support the usual conclusion that maximalism is replacing quiet luxury. Based on The Data Fashion Brief's analysis of Paris Fashion Week SS27, 56 percent of the season's colour palette was still neutral; several houses showed collections that were almost entirely basics, and others went fully bold. The two aesthetics are both on the runway at once, and the change this season is that maximalism got bigger and more visible, not that quiet luxury got smaller.

## Maximalism is bigger, and it is on the runway

Across the 997 Paris looks analysed from 16 shows, 31 percent carried ruffles, fringe, sequins, beading, embroidery, three dimensional flowers, feathers or appliqué, and that reached every one of the 16 houses, with Dior at 70 percent of its looks, Chloé at 69 percent and Isabel Marant at 60 percent. Textured fabrics appeared in 18 percent of looks and metallic finishes reached 14 houses, while the most extreme version, print worn on print, stayed a house story, in 2 percent of looks and led by Dries Van Noten and Miu Miu. Texture play, florals pushed forward as dimensional appliqués, embellished accessories and colourful dressing were among the season's defining trends.

The clearest statement came from Milan, where the Roberto Cavalli collection was reviewed as a burst of fireworks on the runway. The Data Fashion Brief's separate decode of that show puts numbers on it: animal print made up 28 percent of the Cavalli palette, pink 16 percent, red 14 percent, and gold 12 percent, and black fell to 6 percent. In Paris, the houses closest to that register are the ones that opted out of a neutral base: Dries Van Noten at 22 percent neutral with graphic and floral print as its two biggest slices, Balmain at 34 percent neutral with animal print as its single largest slice, and Miu Miu at 35 percent.

*Methodology: The Data Fashion Brief built an AI-assisted tool that scans every look from the Paris Fashion Week SS27 collections and tags it by garment type, fit, fabric, finish, colour and detail. Each trend is measured two ways: how dominant it is within a single designer's own collection, and how present it is across the whole season. Colour shares split every look across up to three colours by how much of the outfit each one covers, so each palette adds up to 100 percent, and neutral means black, white and cream, grey, and brown and beige. The houses covered: Balenciaga, Balmain, Celine, Chanel, Chloé, Dior, Dries Van Noten, Isabel Marant, Lacoste, Lanvin, Loewe, Maison Margiela, Miu Miu, Stella McCartney, Tom Ford and Valentino, 997 looks in total.*

## Colour is wanted, and pink is how it arrived

Colour is the other half of what consumers are asking for, and in Paris it arrived through pink. By palette weight, pink is only 5 percent of the season, but it appears in 8 percent of all looks and in 13 of the 16 houses, which makes it the most widely shared colour in Paris beyond the neutrals. Isabel Marant gave pink 16 percent of its palette, more than three times the season average, in a pale, faded register that matches the reviews describing the collection as faded pastels, while Valentino's 13 percent was fuchsia and cerise. The shade that connects the most houses is ballet pink, in 17 looks across six collections. Green was the season's biggest single colour at 8 percent, concentrated at Lacoste and Loewe, and the colours the press expected most were the ones Paris used least, red at 2 percent of the palette and purple at 1 percent.

## Why maximalism is working now

The runway is responding to a customer who has changed what luxury is for. An industry survey of more than 2,000 luxury clients in the United States and China, published this summer, found the appeal of luxury shifting away from status and visibility towards identity, connection and self-expression, with status signalling and logo recognition in decline in the United States in particular. The same research series reports that 81 percent of luxury customers under 35 name design and creativity as their main reason to buy. A customer buying to express something rather than to signal wealth has a reason to want colour, print and decoration, and quiet luxury, whose whole code was recognition by insiders, has less to offer that customer than it did.

The retail data says the same thing from the other side. Assortment tracking across European brands for summer 2026 recorded sequins up 40 percent and fringe up 34 percent year on year, read by the analysts as a demand for escapism and individuality, and the same report recorded premium basics growing at the same time, T-shirts up 41 percent and denim up 47 percent, which it framed as a season split between minimalist and maximalist brands rather than a swing from one to the other. Consumer search points the same way, with searches for "80s luxury" up 225 percent and "chunky belt" up 65 percent, the signature of an eighties-inspired maximalism, bigger shoulders and chunkier gold jewellery, that the search data names as one of the year's fashion trends.

Price has done some of the work too. Luxury prices rose 61 percent on average between 2019 and 2025, and consultancy forecasts for 2026 describe a sector returning to growth of 3 to 5 percent while warning that those increases left aspirational shoppers feeling let down. A shopper who can no longer justify a plain cashmere piece at a luxury price has little reason to keep dressing in the uniform that justified it, and the houses that are growing with decoration, colour and visible craft are selling that shopper something that looks like what it costs. None of this is new in fashion's history, since periods of restraint have tended to give way to display once the mood that produced them lifts, and the one rigorous attempt to test fashion against the economic cycle, a study of hemlines from 1921 to 2009, found a weak link with a lag of about three years. Three years on from the 2023 peak of quiet luxury is roughly now.

## Quiet luxury has not gone anywhere

The same dataset shows the other customer still being dressed. Black accounted for 21 percent of the Paris palette, white and cream for 17 percent, brown and beige for 11 percent and grey for 7 percent, which puts 56 percent of the season's colour weight in neutrals. Lanvin was 89 percent neutral, with half of its entire palette in black, Celine 81 percent and Balenciaga 74 percent, and those three plus Tom Ford each put black at a third or more of their palettes. Alongside the bold, decorated collections, these were shows built almost entirely on basics, and the season held both without either side looking out of place.

That fits what The Data Fashion Brief's earlier analysis of quiet luxury's customer found: the shopper buying Loro Piana, Brunello Cucinelli or The Row was never buying neutrals as a trend, and is not leaving now that the trend has moved on. The customer who adopted beige as a performance of wealth is the one walking towards colour and decoration, and that shift is what the runway's louder half is answering. Maximalism is not taking quiet luxury's place, it is taking the space the aspirational customer vacated, and the Paris houses have split along exactly that line, some going bold, some staying very basic, very few trying to do both at once.

## Accessories carry the loudest part, starting with sunglasses

Accessories are where the maximalist half of the season is most visible, and where coverage and buyers agree most. Buyers surveyed after the spring shows named belts the key statement piece of the season, big at Celine and triple at Gucci, second only to chunky jewellery and maxi sunglasses, which they described as enduring, and one buyer singled out Celine's round metal sunglasses. Show by show, Celine reworked its oversized oval sunglasses as rimless versions and sent out highlighter yellow and bug eyed styles with enormous belts; Miu Miu showed bug eye frames crossed with a cat eye shape in a parade of colours next to oversized bowling bags; Balmain put sunglasses on many of its models, and charm adorned footwear and vivid floral accessories were picked out across the season's trend coverage.

## What this means for brands and retailers

The season does not support the headline that maximalism has killed quiet luxury. It supports something more useful for a buyer: two customers, both real, both on the runway, with the maximalist one growing. Consumers want colour and decoration again and the collections reflect it, in embellishment at every house in Paris, in pink at 13 of 16, in the volume of eyewear and belts the buyers picked out. At the same time, Lanvin, Celine and Balenciaga are still dressing the quiet luxury shopper in collections that are three quarters neutral or more, because that shopper has not changed.

For brands, the implication is to decide which customer a collection is for and commit, as most Paris houses did, rather than diluting one with the other. For retailers, it argues for carrying both at full strength, the bold, colourful, decorated layer for the customer who has moved on from beige, and the plain, neutral basics in depth for the one who never left. Search data points the same way for now. According to The Data Fashion Brief's analysis of Google Trends, worldwide interest in "quiet luxury" reached its high for the year in May and had fallen to less than a third of that level by August, which reads as the label losing its grip, not the customer. The real test comes when the Spring/Summer 2027 deliveries reach stores next year. One season of runway data is a signal, not a verdict, but the signal is consistent: maximalism is bigger and louder than it was, and quiet luxury is still there beside it.
