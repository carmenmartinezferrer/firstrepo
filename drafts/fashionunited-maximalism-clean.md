3. **Embellishment at every house: the maximalism trend, measured, and its outlook for Fall/Winter 2027**
   Standfirst: Decoration reached all 16 Paris houses analysed and buyers named oversized belts, jewellery and sunglasses the season's statement pieces, while next season's forecasts move the trend from colour to texture.

---

With the Spring/Summer 2027 shows complete and the Fall/Winter 2027 buying season about to open, the industry's read on the season has settled on one word, maximalism, with Roberto Cavalli's Milan show as its loudest example and coverage of Paris describing a turn towards prints, charms, embellishment and colour. The demand is real, and the runway reflects it. Based on The Data Fashion Brief's analysis of Paris Fashion Week SS27, decoration reached every one of the 16 houses analysed, colour arrived as an accent in most of them, and accessories carried the loudest part of the look. The question for buyers is not whether maximalism is here, since the data says it is, but how big it actually got, which version of it is selling, and whether it holds into next season.

## Maximalism is bigger, and it is on the runway

Across the 997 Paris looks analysed from 16 shows, 31 percent carried ruffles, fringe, sequins, beading, embroidery, three dimensional flowers, feathers or appliqué, and that reached every one of the 16 houses, with Dior at 70 percent of its looks, Chloé at 69 percent and Isabel Marant at 60 percent. Textured fabrics appeared in 18 percent of looks and metallic finishes reached 14 houses, while the most extreme version, print worn on print, stayed a house story, in 2 percent of looks and led by Dries Van Noten and Miu Miu. Texture play, florals pushed forward as dimensional appliqués, embellished accessories and colourful dressing were all named among the season's defining trends in the coverage of the Paris shows.

The clearest statement came from Milan, where the Roberto Cavalli collection was reviewed as a burst of fireworks on the runway. The Data Fashion Brief's separate decode of that show puts numbers on it: animal print made up 28 percent of the Cavalli palette, pink 16 percent, red 14 percent and gold 12 percent, and black fell to 6 percent. In Paris, the houses closest to that register are the ones that opted out of a neutral base, Dries Van Noten at 22 percent neutral with graphic and floral print as its two biggest slices, Balmain at 34 percent neutral with animal print as its single largest slice, and Miu Miu at 35 percent. The season had a Cavalli end and a basics end, and most houses sat in between, with decoration on a plainer base.

## Colour is wanted, and pink is how it arrived

Colour is the other half of what consumers are asking for, and in Paris it arrived through pink. By palette weight pink is only 5 percent of the season, but it appears in 8 percent of all looks and in 13 of the 16 houses, which makes it the most widely shared colour in Paris beyond the neutrals. Isabel Marant gave pink 16 percent of its palette, more than three times the season average, in a pale, faded register that matches the reviews describing the collection as faded pastels, while Valentino's 13 percent was fuchsia and cerise. The shade that connects the most houses is ballet pink, in 17 looks across six collections. Green was the season's biggest single colour at 8 percent, concentrated at Lacoste and Loewe, and the colours the press expected most were the ones Paris used least, red at 2 percent of the palette and purple at 1 percent.

## Why maximalism is working now

The runway is responding to a customer who has changed what luxury is for. An industry survey of more than 2,000 luxury clients in the United States and China, published this summer, found the appeal of luxury shifting away from status and visibility towards identity, connection and self-expression, with status signalling and logo recognition in decline in the United States in particular. The same research series reports that 81 percent of luxury customers under 35 name design and creativity as their main reason to buy. A customer buying to express something rather than to signal wealth has a reason to want colour, print and decoration.

The retail data says the same thing from the other side. Assortment tracking across European brands for summer 2026 recorded sequins up 40 percent and fringe up 34 percent year on year, which the analysts read as a demand for escapism and individuality, and the same report recorded premium basics growing at the same time, T-shirts up 41 percent and denim up 47 percent, a season it framed as split between minimalist and maximalist brands rather than swinging from one to the other. Consumer search points the same way. According to The Data Fashion Brief's analysis of Google Trends, worldwide searches for "chunky belt" averaged almost double their level of a year earlier, up 93 percent, and "80s luxury", the shorthand for bigger shoulders and chunkier gold jewellery, rose from almost nothing, up more than 400 percent on a small base. Both terms peaked in the spring and have settled back since, but both closed the year well above where they started.

Price and the economic mood have done some of the work too. Luxury prices rose 61 percent on average between 2019 and 2025, and consultancy forecasts for 2026 describe a sector returning to growth of 3 to 5 percent while warning that those increases left aspirational shoppers feeling let down, at a time when 46 percent of fashion executives surveyed expected conditions to worsen this year. A shopper who can no longer justify a plain cashmere piece at a luxury price has little reason to keep dressing in the uniform that justified it, and the houses growing with decoration, colour and visible craft are selling that shopper something that looks like what it costs. None of this is new in fashion's history, since periods of restraint have tended to give way to display once the mood that produced them lifts, and the one rigorous attempt to test fashion against the economic cycle, a study of hemlines from 1921 to 2009, found a weak link with a lag of about three years. Three years on from the 2023 peak of quiet luxury is roughly now.

The shift is not confined to clothing, and the version of it taking hold in the home is the same one the fabric fairs are showing. The interior designer Barbara Reimelt, speaking to her followers on social media this month, said she had tired of the matched set of curved sofa and curved chairs that has been the look for several seasons and wanted pieces that almost do not go together, a sofa with corners next to a vintage or mid-century chair, because a piece that has had a life before brings a character that new furniture rarely has, and that burgundy, this year's colour for whole rooms and ceilings, works for her only as an accent. Mismatched rather than matched, lived-in rather than new, and one deep colour used sparingly is maximalism as texture and character, not excess, which is exactly the Fall/Winter 2027 reading below. The Data Fashion Brief's own Google Trends data shows worldwide searches for "maximalist interior" running about half as high again as a year earlier on average, on a small base. Beauty is moving the same way, with one search tracker reporting "maximalist makeup" up 96 percent year on year as bold colour replaces the pared-back look, and branding coverage now describes the end of the flat, minimal identity that made luxury houses look interchangeable. The home is usually slower to turn than the wardrobe, so a shift there is a sign of a mood with some duration behind it rather than a single season's styling.

## The quiet customer is still there

None of this means the neutral wardrobe has gone, a point covered in last month's piece on colour and worth restating only briefly. Black, white and cream, grey and brown together still made up 56 percent of the Paris palette, Lanvin was 89 percent neutral, Celine 81 percent and Balenciaga 74 percent, and worldwide search interest in "quiet luxury" peaked in May before falling to about a fifth of that level by late October, below the same weeks a year earlier. The customer who bought plain cashmere as a matter of taste has not changed, and the Paris houses that dress that customer have not changed either. What has changed is the size of the other half of the season.

## Accessories carry the loudest part, starting with sunglasses

Accessories are where the maximalist half of the season is most visible, and where coverage and buyers agree most. Buyers surveyed after the spring shows named belts the key statement piece of the season, big at Celine and triple at Gucci, second only to chunky jewellery and maxi sunglasses, which they described as enduring, and one buyer singled out Celine's round metal sunglasses. Show by show, Celine reworked its oversized oval sunglasses as rimless versions and sent out highlighter yellow and bug eyed styles with enormous belts, Miu Miu showed bug eye frames crossed with a cat eye shape in a parade of colours next to oversized bowling bags, Balmain put sunglasses on many of its models, and charm adorned footwear and vivid floral accessories were picked out across the season's trend coverage.

## What carries into Fall/Winter 2027

The forward signals are already in, because the fabric fairs and the forecasters work a season ahead of the runway, and they agree on the direction while changing its form. At the Paris fabric fair held in September for Autumn/Winter 2027/28, the season was built around material rather than colour, with texture, irregularity and visible craftsmanship presented as the new markers of luxury, a palette of nuanced neutrals, deep purples and bordeaux rather than primary brights, and a "jewellery box" micro-theme of metallic-thread tweeds and sequin-embroidered laces in powdery shades. Two data-led forecasters read the same season the same way. One groups its Fall/Winter 2027 womenswear outlook under a "sensory riot" theme and frames maximalism as a counter-reaction to corporate minimalism and AI-generated imagery, with clashing prints, dramatic fringe and textures a screen cannot reproduce. The other names "max glamour" as a key theme for the season, reports fringe detailing up 17 percent year on year in July 2026 with a further rise forecast for the second quarter of 2027, and expects adoption of large sequins to grow by 23 percent over the next two years.

Read against the Paris data, that is maximalism moving from colour to texture. The Spring/Summer 2027 version was pink, ballet to fuchsia, embellished sandals and bug-eye sunglasses. The Fall/Winter 2027 version the fairs are showing is fringe, fur-effect mass, sequins worked into tweed and lace, and a darker palette of purples, bordeaux and mineral neutrals, which is the same impulse in heavier cloth. The accessories signal supports the continuation: worldwide searches for "chunky belt" rose a second time in the weeks around fashion month, after their spring peak, which is the pattern of a trend being re-bought rather than one fading out. So the trend has the potential to continue, and the data suggests it will, but as a denser, more material-led maximalism than the one that walked in Paris, and one that buyers will see first at the January and February trade fairs and then at the Fall/Winter 2027 shows.

## What this means for brands and retailers

For Spring/Summer 2027 deliveries, the data supports buying the decorated layer with confidence, embellishment at every house, pink as the accent colour, oversized belts, jewellery and eyewear, while keeping plain basics in depth, since the Paris houses themselves split into a bold camp and a basic camp and very few tried to do both at once. For Fall/Winter 2027, the signal is to shift the maximalist budget from bright colour to surface: fringe, sequins on tweed and lace, texture and visible craft, in purples, bordeaux and deep neutrals, with accessories still carrying the volume.

One season of runway data is a signal, but the signal is consistent across the runway, retail assortments, search and now the fabric fairs. Maximalism is bigger and louder than it was, and its next season looks heavier rather than quieter. The first real test comes at the January trade fairs, when buyers commit to it in cloth.
