# Stop Calling Everything a Trend: The Checklist Edition

## Is it a trend, or just one house having a moment?

*Six questions I ask my runway and search data before I call anything real, and the examples that taught me each one.*

Tweed was in 5% of the looks I tagged in Paris this season and in 42% of Chanel's own collection, which is how a fabric ends up on every trend list while being, in practice, one house's signature that a couple of neighbours borrowed.

Every season my inbox fills with lists of trends, and I read each one with the same question, which is how anyone knows. I tag every look from the biggest shows and set the result against a year of Google searches, so I've watched a lot of confident trends fall apart the moment somebody counted them. This is the checklist I actually use, with the examples that taught it to me, and you can run it on any list that lands in your feed before you spend anything on it.

A quick note on how I check, so the examples make sense. Every look is tagged against a fixed taxonomy (garment, fit, fabric, colour, detail), which gives each trend a share within its own house and a share across the whole season, and then I put the strongest candidates side by side on one scale against a full year of Google search data. The full methodology is at the bottom.

## 1. How many houses wear it, and how much of each?

A trend needs both numbers, because either one on its own will mislead you. Tweed is the classic case: 5% of all Paris looks, almost all of it Chanel, with Dior a distant second and only Miu Miu and Valentino touching it after that. PVC is the extreme version, six trench coats and capes at Chloé and nobody else, so however good the coats were, it's one house's idea.

The opposite trap is the thing that's everywhere and barely visible. Denim was in only 4% of Paris looks but reached 63% of the houses, which is light in volume and wide in reach, and that is what a wardrobe basic looks like in data. A real trend looks different: the column was the overall silhouette of 35% of all Paris looks and reached every house, and in New York the midi skirt did the same. So the combination to look for is wide and heavy, and if it's wide but light you're probably looking at a staple, if it's narrow but heavy it's a house signature, and if it's narrow and light it's mostly noise.

Even "every house" needs a second look. The pencil skirt reached all 16 Paris houses and was in 15% of all looks, but Chanel put one in 49% of its collection and Dries Van Noten in 40%, while Lacoste showed exactly one, which makes it a concentrated trend wearing a universal number.

## 2. Does it still win when everything sits on the same scale?

A trend can look strong alone and still barely register next to another candidate, which is the step that finds the story for me. In New York the midi skirt was the more universal silhouette, in every house, but when I put both on Google's own 0 to 100 scale, slip dress ran higher in 50 of the last 53 weeks, and outside the show-season spike (February to May) it averaged 25 against the midi skirt's 9, nearly three times the search interest. The runway and the search data were telling two different stories, and that gap was the finding: the slip dress was the settled, dependable one, and the midi skirt was the one I'd bet on building.

The same logic catches forecasts. A forecast report I read this autumn (Trendalytics, Womenswear Fall/Winter 2027) gave 24 non-colour items one number each, in six different kinds, so a +21% sat next to a +888% on the same slide and no item was measured two ways. Once I grouped the items by the metric they shared and plotted each group on one scale, Heritage Craft and Sensory Riot showed real movement and Veiled Performance mostly didn't, even though all three looked equally strong on the page. The 888%, for funnel neck jackets, was growth among key influencers with no word on the starting point, and 888% from almost nothing is exactly how a funnel neck starts to look like a movement.

## 3. Does it survive after the spike?

Runway season does odd things to search, because everything jumps in the spring and then settles, so the test is what's left once the excitement has gone. The skinny jean is the cleanest example: worldwide search interest jumped sharply in the spring and by early October had fallen back to last year's level, which matches exactly one skinny jean in the whole of Paris, at Tom Ford. The barrel jean is the opposite case, because it peaked in April and has since settled at less than half that peak, but it has stayed higher than the same week a year earlier in every week of the past year, and over the past two months it has run at almost double last year's level. A spike tells you people looked, and the weeks afterwards tell you whether anyone stayed.

## 4. Does a source that doesn't depend on the first one say the same?

When two independent sources land on the same finding, that's a much stronger claim than either alone, and when they only partly overlap I'd rather say exactly how much than round it up to "confirmed". Embellishment is the example from this autumn: 31% of all Paris looks carried it (ruffles, fringe, sequins, beading and similar), at every one of the 16 houses, with Dior at 70% of its own looks, while [Lectra's tracking of summer 2026 retail assortments](https://fashionunited.uk/press/fashion/fashion-trends-2026-between-minimalism-and-maximalism-lectra-presents-a-summer-of-contrasts-and-value/2026061888736) had sequins up 40% and fringe up 34% on last year, and the forecast report put the fringe skirt at the top of its search movers, up 71%. That's three sources with three different methods all pointing the same way.

Silk is a full match, since the forecast calls it the most saturated fabric in luxury and in my data the silk family was in 28% of Paris looks across 15 of the 16 houses. Tweed is the partial one, because "most saturated" sounds like everyone, whereas in my data it's one house plus two or three borrowers.

## 5. Is the season average hiding a few loud houses?

A modest season-wide number can hide a concentrated story, and the press can be responding to the story while the average looks safe. Red at New York Fashion Week was 9% of the season's colour weight, the seventh biggest colour, which reads as nothing special, but Tommy Hilfiger built 16% of its own palette around red, nearly double the season figure, with Conner Ives at 12% and Altuzarra at 10% not far behind.

> **In The Press**
> [Marie Claire](https://www.marieclaire.com/fashion/new-york-fashion-week-street-style-trend-spring-summer-2027-red-color/) ran a whole piece on red taking over NYFW SS27 street style and named Tommy Hilfiger and Tory Burch among the houses pushing it, and [WWD's write-up of Pantone's NYFW colour report](https://wwd.com/fashion-news/fashion-trends/pantone-nyfw-spring-2027-color-trends-1239203317/) leads with saturated primaries, vibrant red and cobalt blue specifically. The overlap I can confirm from my own data is Tommy Hilfiger.

So red wasn't a New York wide trend, it was a real story at a few specific houses, and that's the answer I'd give you rather than quoting the coverage next to the number and leaving you to reconcile them.

## 6. Can the data even say what it is?

Sometimes the honest answer is that the source can't tell me, and I'd rather report that than write around it. Cotton was the second biggest material in Paris, in 20% of looks, but almost all of it is tagged simply as "cotton", so I can't tell you whether it's poplin, denim or a linen blend. Drawstrings, funnel necks and hoods aren't tags in my taxonomy at all, so for those I can only say the forecast claims them and I can't measure them, which isn't the same as the forecast being wrong, and a trend I can't measure goes in the unverified pile.

## So what do I do with this?

Next time a list of trends lands in your feed, these are the questions I'd ask of it before spending anything:

- How many houses show it, and how much of each collection is it?
- Does it still lead on the same scale as its closest rival?
- Is it still there once the post-show spike has passed?
- Does an independent source say the same thing?
- Is the headline number hiding two or three very loud houses?
- Can the source say what the thing actually is?

Something that passes most of these is a trend, and something that passes only one is a moment, which can still be lovely to wear but doesn't need your money (passing the test never means you have to buy it, only that you know why it's everywhere).

My take is that most of what gets called a trend is one house, one month of searches or one number with nothing to compare it to, and I think the more useful skill is spotting the fake ones quickly, which saves you money and a lot of scrolling.

## Methodology

I tagged 16 houses from Paris Fashion Week Spring Summer 2027, 997 looks in total, every show complete: Balenciaga, Balmain, Celine, Chanel, Chloé, Dior, Dries Van Noten, Isabel Marant, Lacoste, Lanvin, Loewe, Maison Margiela, Miu Miu, Stella McCartney, Tom Ford and Valentino. For New York Fashion Week Spring Summer 2027 I tagged 13 houses: Altuzarra, Calvin Klein, Carolina Herrera, Coach, Conner Ives, Cult Gaia, Khaite, Michael Kors, Ralph Lauren, Thom Browne, Tommy Hilfiger, Tory Burch and Ulla Johnson. Every look is tagged against a fixed taxonomy by garment type, fit, fabric, colour and detail, and each trend is reported two ways, as a share within a single house's own collection and as a share across the whole season. Shares of looks overlap and don't add up to 100, because one look can include several pieces and materials.

Search figures are Google Trends, on its own 0 to 100 scale, so two terms plotted together are directly comparable. The skinny jean and barrel jean figures come from worldwide searches from October 2025 to October 2026, compared with the preceding year. The slip dress and midi skirt comparison covers the last 53 weeks, and the "outside the show-season spike" averages leave out February to May.

The forecast figures are as printed in the Trendalytics Womenswear Forecast for Fall/Winter 2027, one number per item, and I'm describing its method as Trendalytics presents it, because the report itself prints only the final figures. The three shape labels (wide and heavy, staple, signature) are my own shorthand for how widely and how heavily something appears, not categories in the data. Embellishment counts any look with ruffles, fringe, sequins, beading, embroidery, three-dimensional flowers, feathers or appliqué. Colour shares use the colour mix method, where each look is split across up to three colours by how much of the outfit each covers.
