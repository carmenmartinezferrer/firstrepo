# Is It a Trend, or Just One House Having a Moment?

## The six questions I ask before anything in my articles gets called a trend

*What my runway and search data taught me about telling what's real from what's loud, with the examples behind each question.*

Tweed was in 5% of the looks I tagged in Paris this season and in 42% of Chanel's own collection, which is how a fabric ends up on every trend list while being one house's signature that a couple of neighbours borrowed.

Every season my inbox fills with lists of trends, and I read each one wondering how anyone knows. I tag every look from the biggest shows and set the result against a year of Google searches, so this is the checklist I actually use, and you can run it on any list in your feed before you spend anything on it. A quick note on the method: every look is tagged against a fixed taxonomy, which gives each trend a share within its own house and across the season, and then I put the strongest candidates on one scale against a year of search data (full methodology at the bottom).

## 1. How many houses wear it, and how much of each?

A trend needs both numbers. Tweed is almost all Chanel, and PVC was six trench coats and capes at Chloé and nobody else, so those are house signatures. Denim was in only 4% of Paris looks but reached 63% of the houses, which is what a wardrobe basic looks like in data, while the column was the overall silhouette of 35% of looks at every house, which is a trend. So wide and heavy is a trend, wide but light is a staple, narrow but heavy is a signature, and narrow and light is mostly noise. Even "every house" needs a second look: the pencil skirt reached all 16 Paris houses and 15% of looks, but Chanel put one in 49% of its collection and Dries Van Noten in 40%, while Lacoste showed exactly one.

## 2. Does it still win on the same scale?

In New York the midi skirt was in every house, but on Google's 0 to 100 scale slip dress ran higher in 50 of the last 53 weeks, and outside the show-season spike (February to May) it averaged 25 against 9, so runway and search disagreed, and that gap was the finding. Forecasts need the same test: a report I read this autumn (Trendalytics, Womenswear Fall/Winter 2027) gave 24 non-colour items one number each, in six different kinds, so a +21% sat next to a +888% with no item measured two ways. Grouped by shared metric, Heritage Craft and Sensory Riot showed real movement and Veiled Performance mostly didn't, and the 888% (funnel neck jackets) was influencer growth from an unstated starting point.

## 3. Does it survive the spike?

Skinny jean searches jumped sharply in the spring and were back at last year's level by early October, which matches one skinny jean in the whole of Paris, at Tom Ford. The barrel jean peaked in April and has settled at less than half that peak, but it has stayed above the same week a year earlier in every week of the past year, so it's maturing rather than fading. A spike tells you people looked, and the weeks afterwards tell you whether anyone stayed.

## 4. Does an independent source say the same?

Two sources agreeing is a stronger claim than either alone, and when they only partly overlap I'd rather say exactly how much. Embellishment was in 31% of Paris looks at all 16 houses (Dior at 70%), [Lectra's retail tracking](https://fashionunited.uk/press/fashion/fashion-trends-2026-between-minimalism-and-maximalism-lectra-presents-a-summer-of-contrasts-and-value/2026061888736) had sequins up 40% and fringe up 34% on last year, and the forecast had fringe skirts up 71% in searches, three methods pointing one way. Silk is a full match, the forecast's most saturated luxury fabric and in 28% of Paris looks across 15 of 16 houses, while tweed is partial, since "most saturated" sounds like everyone and my data says one house plus two or three borrowers.

## 5. Is the average hiding a few loud houses?

Red was 9% of New York's colour weight, the seventh biggest colour, but Tommy Hilfiger built 16% of its palette around it, Conner Ives 12% and Altuzarra 10%.

> **In The Press**
> [Marie Claire](https://www.marieclaire.com/fashion/new-york-fashion-week-street-style-trend-spring-summer-2027-red-color/) ran a piece on red taking over NYFW SS27 street style, naming Tommy Hilfiger and Tory Burch, and [WWD's write-up of Pantone's NYFW report](https://wwd.com/fashion-news/fashion-trends/pantone-nyfw-spring-2027-color-trends-1239203317/) leads with vibrant red and cobalt blue. The overlap I can confirm in my data is Tommy Hilfiger.

So red was a real story at a few specific houses, not a New York wide trend, and I'd rather tell you which it is than quote the coverage next to the number and leave you to reconcile them.

## 6. Can the data even say what it is?

Cotton was the second biggest Paris material at 20% of looks, but almost all of it is tagged simply "cotton", so I can't say whether it's poplin, denim or linen blend. Drawstrings, funnel necks and hoods aren't tags in my taxonomy at all, so for those I can only say the forecast claims them and I can't measure them, and a trend I can't measure goes in the unverified pile.

## So what do I do with this?

Next time a list lands in your feed, ask:

- How many houses show it, and how much of each collection is it?
- Does it still lead on the same scale as its closest rival?
- Is it still there once the post-show spike has passed?
- Does an independent source say the same thing?
- Is the headline number hiding two or three very loud houses?
- Can the source say what the thing actually is?

Something that passes most of these is a trend, and something that passes one is a moment, which can still be lovely to wear but doesn't need your money. My take is that most of what gets called a trend is one house, one month of searches or one number with nothing to compare it to, so spotting the fake ones quickly saves you money and a lot of scrolling.

## Methodology

I tagged 16 houses from Paris Fashion Week Spring Summer 2027, 997 looks in total, every show complete: Balenciaga, Balmain, Celine, Chanel, Chloé, Dior, Dries Van Noten, Isabel Marant, Lacoste, Lanvin, Loewe, Maison Margiela, Miu Miu, Stella McCartney, Tom Ford and Valentino. For New York Fashion Week Spring Summer 2027 I tagged 13 houses: Altuzarra, Calvin Klein, Carolina Herrera, Coach, Conner Ives, Cult Gaia, Khaite, Michael Kors, Ralph Lauren, Thom Browne, Tommy Hilfiger, Tory Burch and Ulla Johnson. Every look is tagged by garment type, fit, fabric, colour and detail, and each trend is reported as a share within a single house's collection and as a share across the season. Shares of looks overlap and don't add up to 100, since one look can include several pieces and materials. Embellishment counts any look with ruffles, fringe, sequins, beading, embroidery, three-dimensional flowers, feathers or appliqué. Colour shares use the colour mix method, where each look is split across up to three colours by how much of the outfit each covers.

Search figures are Google Trends on its own 0 to 100 scale, so two terms plotted together are directly comparable. The skinny and barrel jean figures are worldwide searches from October 2025 to October 2026 against the preceding year, and the slip dress and midi skirt comparison covers the last 53 weeks, with the "outside the spike" averages leaving out February to May.

The forecast figures are as printed in the Trendalytics Womenswear Forecast for Fall/Winter 2027, one number per item, and I describe its method as Trendalytics presents it because the report prints only the final figures. The wide and heavy, staple and signature labels are my own shorthand for how widely and heavily something appears, not categories in the data.
