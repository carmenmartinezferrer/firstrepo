# How I Tell a Long-Term Trend From a Good Season

## The five checks I run before I say something is here to stay, and where my own data runs out

Prada put the midi skirt in 28% of its looks for Spring/Summer 2021 and in 82% of them for Spring/Summer 2027, a six-year climb at one house, and it's the kind of line I'd love to draw for every trend, because one season can only tell you what happened while a long-term trend needs several of them.

A season of runway tagging is a snapshot, so the honest answer to how an analyst spots something that lasts is that the runway alone can't, and I borrow longer clocks: search compared year on year, quarters of sales, and the same house across seasons, then I ask whether they agree. If you're deciding what to build a wardrobe, a buy or a collection around, these are the five checks I use, with the examples I have and the places where I don't have the data yet.

## 1. Does it come back the same week next year?

Search jumps around the shows every year, so a single month tells you nothing, and the fair comparison is each week against the same week twelve months earlier. By that test the barrel jean peaked in April and has settled at less than half that peak, yet it has stayed above the same week a year earlier in every week of the past year and over the past two months has run at almost double last year's level, so it's maturing rather than fading. The skinny jean is the reverse: its search jumped sharply in the spring and by early October was back at last year's level, which is what a moment looks like once the excitement has gone. ## 2. Is there a reason underneath that isn't the look?

A look needs a styling reason, whereas a long-term trend needs a behaviour that explains it, and the clearest one in my data is comfort. Denim has spent several seasons moving from the skinny jean towards looser shapes, wide, baggy, barrel and now relaxed straight, and 77% of the denim pieces in Paris SS27 were cut relaxed. The shape keeps changing and the direction doesn't, which is why Gap, Old Navy, Levi's and American Eagle are all selling a family of fits instead of one silhouette. Quiet luxury shows the other side of the same idea: the customer buying Loro Piana or The Row was never after neutrals and stayed, while the aspirational customer who adopted beige as a performance of wealth is the one walking away after several years of it.

## 3. Does money agree for more than one quarter?

Runways and searches show interest and sales show commitment, so I look for a run of quarters rather than one good one. Gap posted its eleventh consecutive quarter of positive comparable sales this summer, 10% in the second quarter, with denim named as a destination category, and Levi's raised its outlook in July after second quarter revenue rose 8%. The same check works in reverse, because long-term trends go down too: Hermès was growing 17% at the start of 2024 and 18% at the end of it, between 7% and 10% through 2025 and 4% this quarter, which reads as a slope rather than a bad quarter, and it's the slope the rest of luxury had already walked down two years earlier.

## 4. How many seasons has the runway repeated it?

This is the check I'm weakest on, because my own tagging is mostly one season deep, plus a few archive decodes. What I can show is one house: Prada's midi was 28% of a 40-look collection in SS21 and 82% in SS27, so at Prada the midi went from a choice to a rule, but that's a single house, decoded from runway screenshots at two different times, and it says nothing yet about the industry. [NEEDS DATA: the same piece or silhouette tagged across at least three consecutive seasons for several houses, which is what turns a house habit into an industry trend.]

## 5. What would prove me wrong, and when will I check?

The last check is a date, not a number. In my quiet luxury piece I wrote that the direction was colour, called it a signal and not a verdict, and said the September shows would either confirm it at scale or reveal a handful of early bets. Paris then came back with 56% of its colour weight in black, white and cream, grey and brown, so my honest label for colour right now is "growing alongside neutrals", not "replacing them". Writing down what would change your mind, and when you'll look again, is the habit that stops me calling a long-term trend on the strength of one good season.

## So what do I do with this?

Before you build anything around a trend, ask whether it repeats on the same week next year, whether there's a reason underneath that isn't the look, whether sales agree for longer than a quarter, whether more than one season has said it, and what would make you drop it. Something that passes two is interesting and something that passes four is worth planning around, and of everything I've checked the relaxed jean passes the most, with the multi-season gap in check 4 still open.

My take is that a long-term trend is mostly a patience problem, since the data that confirms one takes years to exist and the lists that announce one take a week to write.

## Methodology

I tag every look from the biggest shows against a fixed taxonomy by garment type, fit, fabric, colour and detail, and report each trend as a share within a single house's collection and as a share across the season. The Paris Spring Summer 2027 figures cover 16 houses and 997 looks: Balenciaga, Balmain, Celine, Chanel, Chloé, Dior, Dries Van Noten, Isabel Marant, Lacoste, Lanvin, Loewe, Maison Margiela, Miu Miu, Stella McCartney, Tom Ford and Valentino. Colour weight uses the colour mix method, where each look is split across up to three colours by how much of the outfit each covers, and neutral means black, white and cream, grey, and brown and beige. The Prada figures come from my own decodes of the SS21 show (40 looks) and the SS27 show, counted from runway screenshots, and they are midi share of looks.

Search figures are Google Trends, worldwide, October 2025 to October 2026, each week compared with the same week a year earlier. Sales figures are as the companies report them, each on its own reporting basis (comparable sales for Gap, revenue for Levi's, constant exchange rates for Hermès), so they show direction and are not comparable to the decimal.
