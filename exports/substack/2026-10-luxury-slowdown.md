# Even Hermès slowed down. So what is the luxury customer waiting for?

Subtitle: Results week in one story: the exception stopped being one, Gucci's new designer didn't fix the footfall, and a beauty group outgrew everyone.

Hermès was the one luxury house that kept growing through the slowdown. For two years, while Gucci fell and Louis Vuitton wobbled, Hermès posted a number nobody else could, and the industry told itself the problem was everyone else. This week that stopped. Hermès grew 4.3% in the third quarter, to €4.1bn. It's still growth, and most of the sector would take it. But it's the slowest quarter Hermès has had since the lockdown spring of 2020 (the press calls it "the slowest in six years", which is the same thing), it came in under the 5.2% analysts expected, and the shares fell about 5% on the day. So I did what I do when the exception stops being one: I pulled every luxury result from the week and read them together.

## It's not the bags

The obvious explanation is that people stopped wanting the bags. The data says the opposite. Leather goods, which is the Birkin, the Kelly and everything with a waiting list, grew 10.8% in the quarter. Ready-to-wear fell, for the first time. Watches fell 6%. Greater China was flat over nine months, and the growing happened elsewhere: the Americas up 8.8%, Japan up 9.6%, Europe outside France up 9.5%.

[Chart: chart-hermes-growth.png. Hermès quarterly revenue growth at constant exchange rates, Q1 2024 to Q3 2026. Source: Hermès.]

Put the quarters in a row and the shape is clear. Hermès was growing 17% at the start of 2024 and 18% at the end of it. Through 2025 it sat between 7% and 10%. This year it's 6%, 7%, and now 4.3%. That's not a bad quarter, it's a slope, and it's the same slope the rest of luxury walked down two years earlier. Hermès just had more height to lose. Nine months in, it has sold €12.3bn, up 6%, on a recurring operating margin of 41% at the half year. Nobody at Hermès is in trouble.

My read: the bags are fine. What's slowing is everything around the bags, the clothes, the watches, the second and third purchase a customer makes once she's in the door. And in China she isn't making it.

## The scoreboard

Around Hermès the picture is worse, and reading the results side by side tells you more than any one of them.

[Chart: chart-q3-scoreboard.png. Q3 2026 revenue growth, Puig to Gucci, as each group reports it. Sources: company releases.]

The fastest grower of the week was Puig. A beauty group, up 5.6% like for like, with makeup up 16.1%. Puig owns Charlotte Tilbury, Rabanne and Carolina Herrera, and the makeup did the work. Then Hermès at 4.3%. Then the rest. Prada Group grew 1%. LVMH grew 1% to €18.5bn, but its fashion and leather goods division, which is Louis Vuitton and Dior, fell 5% to €8.2bn, and LVMH named China as the cause; Sephora, up 8%, and jewellery, up 6%, carried the group. Moncler was flat, with the Moncler brand down 1% and Stone Island up 5%. Kering fell 1% to €3.35bn. Miu Miu, the fastest-growing brand in luxury for two years, fell 3% in retail sales, after a 29% jump in the same quarter last year and 41% over the first nine months of 2025 (this year's nine months: up 1% to €1.15bn). And Gucci fell 6% to €1.29bn.

One note on the chart: each bar is the growth measure each group reports (constant exchange rates, comparable, organic, like for like), so they're comparable in direction, not to the decimal.

I keep coming back to Puig. When a makeup company outgrows Hermès, the customer hasn't disappeared. She's spending less per visit. My read is that she's spending it on the one thing from the luxury world she can still justify at full price.

## Gucci changed everything. Sales fell anyway

Gucci is the house that tried hardest to fix itself, which makes it the most useful case in the set.

Demna started in July 2025. The "La Famiglia" lookbook came in September, the first runway show in Milan in February 2026, and the first full collection reached stores from mid-July. Kering changed its CEO too, with Luca de Meo in the job since September 2025. And for a year the plan worked, on paper: the decline narrowed every quarter, from -25% in the first half of 2025 to -2% in the second quarter of this year, the quarter the first collection reached stores.

[Chart: chart-gucci-quarters.png. Gucci comparable revenue growth by quarter, Q1 2025 to Q3 2026. Source: Kering.]

Then this quarter, -6% again. Kering's explanation was "a less pronounced impact from the introduction of new collections". My paraphrase: the newness wore off. The number that matters sits underneath. Sales in Gucci's own stores fell 9%, while wholesale rose 35%. Conversion, average price and basket size all improved. Traffic didn't, with Kering pointing to lower footfall in mainland China, Japan and the US. So the product converts the people who walk in. Fewer people are walking in.

Kering's response was to raise its 2026 net store-closure target from 100 to at least 125. That's a group target, not Gucci's alone, and 105 are already done over nine months (128 closed, 23 opened). It gave no date for a return to growth. The shares fell 11 to 12% on the day.

My read: a creative director can fix the clothes. He can't fix the footfall. The collection was the easiest part of the turnaround, and it's done. What's left is harder.

## China is buying Chinese

Every one of these results names China, so it's worth being precise about what's happening there, because the money hasn't gone. It's moving.

Louis Vuitton's Chinese clients were down mid-teens in the quarter. Bloomberg's data has mainland sales at houses including Hermès and Cartier down by as much as double digits in August and September. On Huaihai Road in Shanghai, where Cartier, Tiffany and Gucci have closed doors, Chinese brands have moved in. And the Chinese personal luxury market as a whole has now shrunk two years in a row.

[Chart: chart-laopu-gold.png. Laopu Gold revenue, 2024 and 2025. Source: Luxury Tribune, Marketing to China (trade press).]

The clearest example is Laopu Gold, a Beijing jeweller that sells heritage gold pieces at fixed prices. Trade press puts its revenue at about RMB 8.5bn in 2024 and RMB 27.3bn in 2025, roughly three times in a year. Those are reported figures, not audited accounts, and the line that its share price is up more than twentyfold since the June 2024 listing comes from a single analyst, so treat the scale as indicative. Songmont, a bag brand, is growing mainly online at a fraction of European prices. None of them is Hermès-sized. But the Chinese customer who is still spending on luxury is increasingly spending it on something designed, made and priced at home, and the Western houses are reporting the gap.

## Margin over growth

Look at what the houses are doing in response, rather than what they're saying, and one choice shows up everywhere.

Kering is closing at least 125 stores this year. Hermès slowed to 4.3% and kept a 41% margin. LVMH's fashion division shrank 5% and the group protected its profit. Even outside luxury the mood is the same. In Modaes' 2026 barometer of about 300 Spanish fashion companies, run with Simon-Kucher and published on 22 October, 53% now put profitability first, up from 45% last year. Growth-first fell from 42% to 36%. And 53% plan to raise prices, 14 points more than a year ago. In the same study, 61% of shoppers rank price among their top three buying criteria. Companies and customers are walking in opposite directions.

Nobody is chasing volume. Everyone is defending the margin. It's a rational choice for a quarter, maybe for a year. The risk is what Bain-Altagamma found in November: years of aggressive price increases left a large part of the customer base feeling priced out. Its June update expects personal luxury goods to grow just 2 to 4% in 2026, with the growth concentrated at the top, Europe the weak link and China fragile.

My read: fashion walked away from its entry price. The card holder, the belt, the sunglasses, the things that make the Birkin customer ten years later. Beauty didn't, and beauty is growing.

## My take

The luxury customer isn't waiting for a new designer. Gucci proved that this quarter. She isn't waiting for the bags to get better either; Hermès' leather business says they're fine. She's waiting for a reason to pay the price, and right now beauty gives her one at €40 that fashion hasn't at €4,000.

Which leaves luxury choosing to be smaller: fewer stores, higher prices, fewer buyers, and a China building its own version of the thing Europe used to sell it. That's a very good business for Hermès and a difficult one for everyone else.

The question for the next two quarters is simple. When the entry price comes down, through outlets, through beauty, through resale, does she come back? Or has she found a cheaper reason to feel the same way?
